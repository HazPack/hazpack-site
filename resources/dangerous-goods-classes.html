Dangerous Goods Classes

Dangerous goods are sorted into nine hazard classes, some with divisions. The class tells everyone handling the package what kind of hazard it presents.

The nine classes

The same nine-class system is used for air, ocean and US ground transport. Some classes are split into divisions that describe the specific hazard.

ClassNameDivisionsExamples
1Explosives1.1 mass explosion hazard; 1.2 projection hazard; 1.3 fire hazard with minor blast or projection; 1.4 no significant hazard; 1.5 very insensitive; 1.6 extremely insensitiveFireworks, ammunition, flares
2Gases2.1 flammable gas; 2.2 non-flammable, non-toxic gas; 2.3 toxic gasPropane, compressed air, aerosols, chlorine
3Flammable liquidsNo divisionsPaint, gasoline, alcohol, adhesives, perfume
4Flammable solids and reactive substances4.1 flammable solids; 4.2 spontaneously combustible; 4.3 dangerous when wetMatches, certain metal powders, calcium carbide
5Oxidizers and organic peroxides5.1 oxidizing substances; 5.2 organic peroxidesPool chemicals, hydrogen peroxide solutions, bleaching agents
6Toxic and infectious substances6.1 toxic substances; 6.2 infectious substancesPesticides, certain lab samples and clinical specimens
7Radioactive materialNo divisionsCertain medical and industrial isotopes
8CorrosivesNo divisionsAcids, battery fluid, drain cleaners
9Miscellaneous dangerous goodsNo divisionsLithium batteries, dry ice, magnetized materials, environmentally hazardous substances

Packing groups

For many classes, a material is also assigned a packing group that shows how dangerous it is. Packing group I indicates great danger, II medium danger and III minor danger. The packing group affects which packaging can be used and the quantity limits that apply.

Subsidiary risks

Some materials present more than one hazard, for example a liquid that is both flammable and corrosive. The regulations assign a primary class and, where needed, one or more subsidiary hazards. Each must be shown on the paperwork and the package labels.

Why classification comes first

Everything else in the shipment depends on getting the classification right: the packaging you can use, the labels and marks, the paperwork, and the limits for each mode of transport. Classification is the shipper's responsibility. A Safety Data Sheet is a starting point, but it is not a substitute for classification against the regulations.
