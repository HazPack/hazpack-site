Shipping with Dry Ice

Dry ice keeps shipments cold, and it is also a regulated dangerous good. Here is what that means for your packaging, marks and paperwork.

Why dry ice is regulated

Dry ice is solid carbon dioxide. It is very cold, about −78.5 °C (−109 °F), and it turns straight into carbon dioxide gas as it warms. That creates three risks in transport:

  • Pressure. In a sealed container the gas has nowhere to go, and the package can burst.
  • Breathing. In a closed space, such as an aircraft hold, a truck or a container, the gas can displace oxygen.
  • Cold burns. Touching it with bare skin can cause injury.

For these reasons dry ice is classed as a Class 9 (miscellaneous) dangerous good, identification number UN1845, proper shipping name "Dry ice" or "Carbon dioxide, solid".

Packaging

  • The packaging must let the gas escape. Never seal dry ice in an airtight container.
  • Use strong, insulated packaging that still holds together as the dry ice shrinks.
  • Plan the amount of dry ice around the transit time, including delays.

Marks and labels (by air)

For air, the IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations (Packing Instruction 954) set the rules. Each package with dry ice generally needs:

  • UN1845 and the proper shipping name
  • The net weight of dry ice in kilograms
  • The shipper's and consignee's names and addresses
  • The Class 9 hazard label

If packages with dry ice go into an overpack, the overpack must also show the total net weight of dry ice inside it.

By air, a package can hold up to 200 kg of dry ice, on passenger and cargo aircraft. Many airlines set lower limits, and the shipper must arrange every dry ice shipment with the airline in advance so it can plan ventilation in the hold.

Paperwork

When dry ice is only used to keep goods that are not dangerous cold, a Shipper's Declaration for Dangerous Goods is usually not needed by air. The air waybill must still describe the dry ice: UN1845, the proper shipping name, the number of packages and the net weight of dry ice in each package. If the package also holds other dangerous goods, the dry ice goes on the Dangerous Goods Declaration with them.

Ocean and ground

  • Ocean: the IMDG Code applies. A container or vehicle cooled with dry ice also needs a warning mark on its doors, because of the risk of breathing in the gas.
  • US ground: 49 CFR covers dry ice. The requirements are lighter than for air, but parcel carriers usually still want UN1845, "Dry ice" and the net weight marked on the box.

Things to watch

  • Old marks and labels left on a reused box. Remove or cover anything that no longer applies.
  • A net weight that does not match the paperwork.
  • Coolers taped shut so tightly that gas cannot escape.

Rules differ by mode of transport and are updated regularly. Always check the current edition of the regulations that apply to your shipment, or ask us.
