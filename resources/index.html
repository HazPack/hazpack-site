Common questions

Quick answers for shippers

Short answers to the questions we hear most. Each one links to the full guide.

  1. What is the difference between hazmat and dangerous goods?Basics

    They describe the same thing from different rulebooks. Hazmat is the term in US regulations. Dangerous goods is the term in the international rules for air (IATA, ICAO) and ocean (IMDG Code). One shipment can fall under more than one set of rules, depending on how it travels.

    Read the full guide: What is Hazmat? →
  2. Who regulates dangerous goods shipments?Basics

    PHMSA, part of the US DOT, writes the Hazardous Materials Regulations in 49 CFR Parts 171 to 180. The FAA, FMCSA, FRA and US Coast Guard enforce them for air, highway, rail and water. ICAO and IATA set the international air rules, and the IMO publishes the IMDG Code for ocean.

    Read the full guide: What is Hazmat? →
  3. Are everyday products like perfume, paint or aerosols regulated?Basics

    Often, yes. Paint, aerosols, perfume, adhesives, cleaning products, batteries and even dry ice can be hazardous materials in transport, not only fuels and industrial chemicals.

    Read the full guide: What is Hazmat? →
  4. What are the nine hazard classes?Basics

    1 Explosives, 2 Gases, 3 Flammable liquids, 4 Flammable solids and reactive substances, 5 Oxidizers and organic peroxides, 6 Toxic and infectious substances, 7 Radioactive material, 8 Corrosives, 9 Miscellaneous dangerous goods. Some classes are split into divisions.

    Read the full guide: Dangerous Goods Classes →
  5. What does the packing group mean?BasicsPaperwork

    It shows how dangerous a material is within its class: packing group I is great danger, II medium and III minor. It affects which packaging you can use and the quantity limits that apply.

    Read the full guide: Dangerous Goods Classes →
  6. Is a Safety Data Sheet enough to classify my product?Basics

    No. A Safety Data Sheet is a starting point, but it is not a substitute for classifying the product against the regulations. Classification is the shipper's responsibility, and everything else in the shipment depends on it.

    Read the full guide: Dangerous Goods Classes →
  7. Do air and ocean use the same hazard classes?AirOcean

    Yes. The same nine-class system is used for air, ocean and US ground transport. What changes between modes is the detail: packaging, quantity limits and paperwork.

    Read the full guide: Dangerous Goods Classes →
  8. What is a Dangerous Goods Declaration?PaperworkAirOcean

    The document where the shipper identifies the dangerous goods and certifies they are properly classified, packaged, marked and labeled. For air it is the Shipper's Declaration for Dangerous Goods, for ocean the Dangerous Goods Declaration, and for US ground the shipping paper.

    Read the full guide: Dangerous Goods Declarations →
  9. Who can sign a dangerous goods declaration?Paperwork

    The shipper is responsible for the declaration, and the person who signs it must be trained. Carriers and emergency responders rely on what it says.

    Read the full guide: Dangerous Goods Declarations →
  10. What mistakes on a declaration cause delays?Paperwork

    The usual ones: a wrong or incomplete proper shipping name, a missing packing group or subsidiary hazard, quantities that do not match the package, no staffed emergency number, an unsigned declaration, or labels that do not agree with the paperwork.

    Read the full guide: Dangerous Goods Declarations →
  11. How often does dangerous goods training need to be renewed?BasicsAir

    Under US rules, hazmat employees must be trained within 90 days of starting the job function and retrained at least every three years. For air shipments, IATA requires recurrent training at least every 24 months.

    Read the full guide: What is Hazmat? →
  12. Which UN number applies to my lithium batteries?Lithium batteries

    UN3480 for lithium ion batteries on their own, UN3481 when contained in or packed with equipment. UN3090 for lithium metal batteries on their own, UN3091 when contained in or packed with equipment.

    Read the full guide: Lithium Batteries →
  13. Can lithium ion batteries ship on passenger aircraft?Lithium batteriesAir

    Not when shipped on their own: lithium ion (UN3480) and lithium metal (UN3090) batteries on their own may only go on cargo aircraft. Batteries packed with or inside equipment can go on passenger aircraft within limits. By air, lithium ion batteries on their own or packed with equipment must be at no more than 30% state of charge.

    Read the full guide: Lithium Batteries →
  14. What is UN 38.3?Lithium batteries

    The test series in the UN Manual of Tests and Criteria that every lithium battery type must pass before it can be transported. Shippers are generally expected to be able to provide a test summary.

    Read the full guide: Lithium Batteries →
  15. Can I ship damaged or recalled batteries?Lithium batteriesAir

    Damaged, defective and recalled batteries are under special rules, and many are forbidden from air transport altogether. Do not ship them without specialist guidance.

    Read the full guide: Lithium Batteries →
  16. What is an overpack, and how is it marked?Paperwork

    An enclosure one shipper uses to hold one or more complete packages as one unit, such as a shrink-wrapped pallet. If the markings and labels inside are not visible, the overpack must be marked OVERPACK and repeat them, except the UN packaging specification marks.

    Read the full guide: What is an Overpack →
  17. Which rules apply to ocean shipments?Ocean

    The IMDG Code, published by the International Maritime Organization. In the US, the Coast Guard enforces the rules for transport by water, and the shipment needs a Dangerous Goods Declaration.

    Read the full guide: What is Hazmat? →
  18. What happens if a shipment is not compliant?Basics

    It can be refused or held at the airport, port or terminal, returned or repackaged at your cost, and delayed. Agencies can also issue civil penalties per violation, often per day it continues.

    Read the full guide: Enforcement and Penalties →
  19. Is dry ice a dangerous good?Dry ice

    Yes. Dry ice (UN1845) is Class 9. It turns into carbon dioxide gas, which can burst a sealed package and displace oxygen in a closed space, so it has its own packaging, marking and labeling rules.

    Read the full guide: Shipping with Dry Ice →
  20. Do I need a Dangerous Goods Declaration to ship dry ice by air?Dry icePaperwork

    Usually not, if the dry ice only keeps non-dangerous goods cold. The air waybill must still show UN1845, the proper shipping name, the number of packages and the net weight of dry ice in each package, and each box must be marked and labeled. If other dangerous goods are in the package, a declaration is needed.

    Read the full guide: Shipping with Dry Ice →
